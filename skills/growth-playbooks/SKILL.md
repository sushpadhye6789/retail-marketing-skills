# Growth Playbooks

Famous brand growth stories get told as trivia — "Dropbox did a referral program" — when the actually useful thing is the **mechanic underneath**: what loop made growth compound instead of needing to be re-earned every period. This skill catalogs those mechanics, grounded in what the brand actually did (not invented statistics), translated for retail, and updated for what changes when a meaningful share of discovery and purchase now runs through a customer's own AI agent rather than a human browsing a page.

## Grounding Rule

Every entry describes a real, publicly documented mechanic. When citing a specific number (Dropbox's referral-driven growth, PayPal's early referral bonus), only use figures that are widely and consistently reported — don't invent or round-fabricate a stat to make an entry more persuasive. If a mechanic is well-known but the numbers aren't reliably documented, describe the mechanic and say so, rather than manufacturing a metric. Applying a playbook to the user's business is a hypothesis to test (route it through `compound-marketing`'s Brief stage), not a guaranteed outcome — the original brand's execution, timing, and market mattered as much as the mechanic itself.

## The AI-Era Delta

This is what most "growth hacking" references miss — they're frozen at a pre-LLM internet. Five shifts change how these mechanics play out now, and they cut across almost every entry in the catalog:

1. **Agentic discovery.** A growing share of product discovery and comparison now happens through a customer's own AI agent (ChatGPT shopping, Perplexity, Amazon Rufus, Claude), not a human scrolling search results. Loops built purely for human eyeballs (SEO copy stuffed with keywords, a referral link that only works in a human's browser session) need an agent-legible counterpart — see `ai-seo` for the mechanics of being citable to an agent, not just rankable to a crawler.
2. **AI content is now commodity-cheap.** Content and SEO flywheels that used to win on volume now compete against an internet full of AI-generated copy. The moat shifts to what AI can't fabricate cheaply: real first-party proof (verified reviews, UGC, community discussion, proprietary data) — not more content.
3. **Authenticity is now a premium, not a baseline.** As generic AI content floods every channel, deliberately human, unscalable-feeling brand moments (a handwritten note, an unhinged mascot account, a founder video) read as more valuable than they did a decade ago, precisely because they're harder to fake at scale. Use AI to remove the *operational* friction around these moments (sourcing, tagging, logistics) — never to generate the moment itself.
4. **Every data flywheel is now also a model flywheel.** First-party data captured through loyalty, community, or personalization doesn't just improve the next customer's experience anymore — it can also train the recommendation/personalization model behind it. That's a second, often-overlooked compounding return on the same data collection effort.
5. **Scarcity mechanics now have to defend against bots.** Drop marketing (limited releases, raffles, queues) worked on the assumption that demand was human and roughly fair. AI purchasing agents change that math — queue and access design now needs an explicit anti-bot / proof-of-humanity layer, or the scarcity mechanic just transfers the prize to whoever has the best bot.

Apply these five as a lens across every entry below, not just where called out explicitly.

## How to Use This Skill

1. **Start from the outcome, not the brand name.** Don't reach for "let's do a Supreme-style drop" because it's memorable — reach for "we need to convert first-time buyers into repeat buyers" and let that point you to the right category below (loyalty/membership, not scarcity).
2. **Check retail fit before adopting a mechanic wholesale.** Several playbooks below were built for SaaS, marketplaces, or high-frequency digital products — see each entry's Retail Translation for what has to change (durable goods have different repeat-purchase economics than a subscription app; a physical store adds a channel a pure-digital brand never had to coordinate).
3. **Check `marketing-strategy`'s brand tier and distribution model first** — a scarcity/drop mechanic that works for a hype streetwear brand can actively damage a mass-market or budget-tier retailer's positioning; a no-loyalty-program stance that works for Trader Joe's-style word-of-mouth needs real product quality and price integrity to back it up.
4. **Route the actual work through `compound-marketing`.** Picking a playbook is the Brief stage's input, not the whole job — draft, execute, analyse, optimise, and compound the result like any other unit of work, so you find out whether it actually worked for your business rather than assuming it did because it worked for the brand you borrowed it from.
5. **If it needs to run on a schedule**, hand the recurring mechanics off to `marketing-loops` (e.g., a referral-loop nudge, a loyalty-tier renewal check) rather than re-deciding whether to run it every time.

## Common Mistakes

- **Copying the surface tactic, not the underlying loop.** A referral program without a genuine reason to share (a real discount, a real perk) is just a discount code with extra steps — the loop compounds because both sides get something real, not because "referral programs work."
- **Ignoring brand tier and distribution model.** A premium/luxury brand running an aggressive PayPal-style "pay people to sign up" mechanic undercuts the exact positioning it's trying to build. Check `marketing-strategy` Section 14 before adopting anything scarcity- or discount-flavored.
- **Treating AI content as a shortcut to the content flywheel.** Publishing AI-generated volume to chase an old SEO playbook is exactly the failure mode Delta #2 above describes — it competes against an internet that already has more of that than anyone can read.
- **Running a scarcity mechanic with no bot defense** and being surprised when human customers can't get in — see Delta #5.
- **Running a scarcity/drop mechanic that isn't real** — quantity or timing claims about a drop need to be genuine; see `offers`' scarcity-urgency guidance and `compliance` before publishing manufactured urgency.
- **Fabricating a growth number** because the real brand's stat isn't confirmable. Describe the mechanic; don't invent the metric.
