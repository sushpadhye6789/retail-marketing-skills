# Eli Weiss (with Val Geisler)

**Lens:** The post-purchase experience — the emails and texts sent *after* someone buys — determines whether they become a repeat customer, and most brands treat that window as a marketing-blast opportunity instead of the customer-experience moment that actually earns retention.

## Public framework

Weiss (VP Advocacy at Yotpo, previously led CX and Retention at OLIPOP and Jones Road Beauty, consulted with 100+ DTC brands) built his retention practice on a core reframe: post-purchase emails and SMS are **customer experience touchpoints first, marketing messages second**. His public writing (his newsletter, interviews) argues that most retention teams over-focus on acquisition-style tactics applied to existing customers, missing that the post-purchase window is where trust and repeat-purchase intent actually get built or lost. **Val Geisler** (named alongside Weiss in the source directory) is a separately well-known figure in the same ecommerce email/retention space — not independently researched in this pass; treat any claim specific to her work as unverified until researched directly.

## Retail application

Directly extends this repo's `emails`/`sms` and `loyalty`/`churn-prevention` skills: Weiss's core argument — that the sequence right after a purchase is a CX design problem, not just a marketing automation to set and forget — reinforces Joey Coleman's First 100 Days lens (see his entry in this catalog) with ecommerce-specific tactical grounding (post-purchase flow structure, not general customer-relationship phases).

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** Auditing whether a post-purchase email/SMS sequence reads as customer care or as continued sales pressure; redesigning post-purchase flows around the customer's actual needs (order status, usage guidance, genuine check-ins) rather than pure upsell messaging.
**Blind spots:** Built specifically from DTC/ecommerce brand experience (OLIPOP, Jones Road) — the CX-first principle generalizes broadly, but specific flow-timing tactics are most directly applicable to consumable/repeat-purchase categories rather than infrequent big-ticket purchases.
