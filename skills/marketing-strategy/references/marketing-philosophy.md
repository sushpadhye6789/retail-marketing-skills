# Marketing Philosophy: How to Decide When Priorities Compete

Section 12 (Strategic Priorities) and `prioritization.md` tell you *how to score and cut* a priorities list. This reference is one level up: the lenses that decide what even gets scored, and how to hold two of them when they actively disagree with each other.

**The short version:** most of a plan's outcome comes from a narrow slice of it, but "narrow" and "reach as many buyers as possible" pull in opposite directions — the resolution isn't picking a side, it's splitting effort roughly 60:40 between them, aiming the reach side at a handful of specific buying moments, and then forcing every resulting list down to three things before starting work.

## The Frameworks

**Pareto (80/20).** Most of the outcome comes from a small slice of the inputs. Applied as a filter, not a statistic: before committing to a plan, ask "which 20% of this actually drives 80% of the result?" and cut the rest, or at minimum sequence it last.

**How Brands Grow (Byron Sharp).** Growth comes mainly from penetration — reaching more buyers, most of whom are light or non-buyers of the category — not from deepening loyalty among existing heavy users. See `marketing-council/references/advisors/byron-sharp.md` for the full framework as applied elsewhere in this repo.

**Binet & Field's 60:40 brand:performance split.** Roughly 60% of budget and effort toward long-term brand-building (broad reach, emotional, penetration-focused) and 40% toward short-term performance/activation (narrow, efficient, response-focused).

**Category Entry Points (CEPs).** The situations, needs, or moments that trigger a buyer to think of a brand.

**The top-3 rule.** When there's a long list of things to do — which is most of the time — force a hard cut to the top 3 by expected impact. Everything else is backlog, not "also happening."

## Where Pareto and Sharp Disagree

These two don't sit quietly next to each other. Pareto says focus narrow: find the small slice that matters and starve everything else of attention. Sharp says the opposite: growth requires reaching *broadly*, including buyers who don't look like your best customers yet, because most of a category's future revenue sits with people who aren't buying much from anyone right now. Applied naively, Pareto argues for narrowing a retailer's marketing to its best customers and highest-margin lines; Sharp argues that's close to how a brand caps its own growth. Neither is wrong — they're answering different questions. Naming this tension explicitly matters more than smoothing it over, because a plan that quietly picks one without saying so will read as internally inconsistent the moment someone asks "then why are we also doing X."

## The Resolution: 60:40

Binet & Field's split is the practical answer to that tension, not a compromise that waters both down. Roughly 60% of budget and effort goes to broad, brand-building, penetration-focused work — the Sharp side, reaching light and non-buyers, building the mental availability that pays off over quarters and years. Roughly 40% goes to narrow, efficient, response-focused performance work — the Pareto side, harvesting demand that already exists at the best possible efficiency.

The frameworks aren't actually in conflict once you see it this way: **Pareto tells you where to focus *within* each bucket — which 20% of the brand work matters most, which 20% of the performance channels earn their keep — not whether to have both buckets in the first place.** Skipping straight to "focus on the top 20%" without first deciding the 60:40 split is how a plan ends up all-performance, all-narrow, and quietly capped on growth.

## Category Entry Points: The Connective Layer

CEPs are what make "reach broadly" tractable instead of a mandate to try to be everywhere. You still reach broadly — that's the Sharp half — but you focus the actual creative and media against the specific situations, needs, and moments that matter most for the category, rather than attempting to own every possible entry point at once. A retailer doesn't need a message for every conceivable reason someone might think of the category; it needs strong, repeated presence at the 3-5 entry points that carry the most volume and the most category-relevant search/consideration behavior. This is Pareto's logic (which 20% of entry points drive 80% of category consideration) applied inside Sharp's mandate (reach the buyers who aren't already loyal) — the two frameworks meeting in one tactical decision instead of staying at odds.

## The Daily Discipline: Top 3

Frameworks resolve strategy; they don't run a Tuesday. When a list of "things we could do" gets long — new campaign ideas, channel tests, creative variants, a backlog of CRO fixes — force a hard cut to the top 3 by expected impact before starting anything. Everything else moves to backlog, not to "also happening in parallel." This is the everyday-use version of the same instinct behind Pareto and the 60:40 split: most plans fail not from picking the wrong priority, but from refusing to admit that everything else on the list is, in fact, not a priority right now.

## Applying This

- **Section 12 (Strategic Priorities):** use the 60:40 split as a sense-check on a draft priorities list — does it read as 100% performance/narrow, or 100% brand/broad? Either extreme is a signal, not necessarily a mistake, but it should be a deliberate choice, not a default.
- **`budget-allocation`:** the 60:40 split is a starting allocation heuristic, not a rule to apply blind — a brand-new store with zero mental availability skews further toward the 60 (or beyond); a mature brand milking a saturated category can lean further toward the 40.
- **`marketing-plan`'s retail-funnel staging:** CEPs are most useful when deciding what Awareness-stage creative and media actually say, since that's the stage where "reach broadly, but about the moments that matter" has to become an actual brief.
- **Any prioritized list, anywhere in this repo's workflow:** apply the top-3 rule before treating a brainstormed list as a plan.
