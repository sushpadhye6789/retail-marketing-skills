# Storytelling

You help users construct a narrative arc — for the brand, a founder, a product, or a customer transformation — that other skills then execute into a finished page, video, campaign, or post. This skill builds the story's *structure*; it doesn't write the final copy.

## Before Starting

**Check for product marketing context first:**
Also check `.agents/marketing-learnings.md` if it exists — past entries tagged to this channel capture what already worked or failed; apply that before drafting from scratch (see `compound-marketing`).
If `.agents/marketing-strategy.md` exists, read it — it's the raw material this skill shapes into a story:
- **Section 4 (Problems & Pain Points)** is the story's problem/tension.
- **Section 6 (Differentiation)** and **Section 8 (Switching Dynamics)** are the guide's plan and the stakes.
- **Section 9 (Customer Language)** is how the story should actually sound, in the audience's own words, not marketing-speak.
- **Section 14 (Brand Tier)** — see `marketing-strategy/references/brand-tier-guide.md`; story pacing and tone should match tier (see below).

Gather this context (ask if not provided):

### 1. Story Type
- Brand story, founder/origin story, product story, or customer transformation/case study?
- Where will it live — a page, a video script, a campaign, a press narrative? (Determines which skill executes the finished version.)

### 2. Raw Material
- For a founder/origin story: what actually happened — the real problem the founder hit, the real insight, not a manufactured myth.
- For a customer story: a real customer, with their permission, not a composite or invented example (see `compliance` on testimonial authenticity).

## Core Narrative Frameworks

### Customer-as-Hero, Brand-as-Guide

The most broadly useful structure for brand and product storytelling, adapted from the "StoryBrand" framework that's become a standard reference point in marketing narrative work (paraphrased here, not quoted — cite Donald Miller's *Building a StoryBrand* directly if the user wants the primary source).

The core inversion this framework insists on: **the customer is the hero of the story, not the brand.** The brand plays the guide — the character who has a plan and helps the hero succeed, the Yoda to the customer's Luke Skywalker, not the hero itself. This maps directly onto the marketing-strategy sections above:

1. **A character has a problem** — Section 4 (Problems & Pain Points)
2. **They meet a guide** (the brand) **who has a plan** — Section 6 (Differentiation), framed as *guidance*, not a sales pitch
3. **The guide calls them to action** — the actual CTA
4. **What's at stake** — Section 8 (Switching Dynamics): success if they act, failure/continued pain if they don't

The most common failure mode is inverting this — making the brand the hero ("we're the best at X") instead of the guide, which reads as self-congratulatory rather than helpful.

### Before-After-Bridge (the compressed version)

`ads` and `offers` already use Before-After-Bridge as a copy formula (current painful state → desired future state → the product as bridge) — it's the same narrative shape as the hero framework above, compressed to fit a headline or short ad instead of a full page or video. Use the hero framework for anything with room to build a real arc; use BAB when space is tight.

### Origin/Founder Story Structure

For an About page, PR narrative, or brand campaign built around why the business exists:

1. **The moment** — the specific instance the founder hit the problem personally (specific and concrete beats vague — "I spent three weekends trying to X" beats "I noticed a gap in the market")
2. **The insight** — what they realized that existing solutions were missing
3. **The decision** — why they decided to build the solution themselves, and what that first version actually looked like
4. **The mission** — how the personal origin connects to what the business stands for today, tying back to `marketing-strategy` Section 6 (Differentiation)

Resist manufacturing a tidier story than what actually happened — a genuine, slightly messy origin story reads as more credible than a polished myth, and inconsistencies get noticed.

### Customer Transformation / Case Study Structure

1. **Before state** — the customer's situation and pain, in their own words if possible (see `customer-research`'s extraction methods for sourcing this)
2. **Catalyst** — what made them look for a solution now, specifically
3. **The journey** — what they tried, why it worked (or didn't, for a comparison), briefly — this isn't the focus, don't over-invest here
4. **After state** — the concrete outcome, with real numbers/specifics where available
5. **Proof** — what backs up the after state (a metric, a quote, a visible result)

Feeds directly into `marketing-strategy` Section 11 (Proof Points) and `customer-research`'s win/loss and NPS extraction work as source material.

This skill builds the arc; these skills execute it:

- **copywriting**: page-level execution (About pages, homepage narrative sections, landing pages)
- **video**: script and production execution
- **social**: carousel/post-level narrative — see `social/references/carousel-frameworks.md`'s five slide-by-slide architectures, which are this skill's structures compressed further for a swipeable format
- **tentpole-campaign**: the story a major brand campaign is built around
- **public-relations**: press narrative and pitch angle

## Common Mistakes

- **Making the brand the hero instead of the guide** — see Customer-as-Hero above; the single most common storytelling mistake in marketing.
- **No real stakes or tension** — a story where nothing is at risk if the customer doesn't act isn't a story, it's a description.
- **Fabricating or exaggerating a customer story** — see `compliance`'s testimonial-authenticity principle; a story built on a real customer's real words beats a more polished invented one, and the invented one carries real compliance risk.
- **A story disconnected from actual positioning** — if the narrative doesn't trace back to `marketing-strategy` Sections 4/6/8, it's decoration, not strategy.
- **Manufacturing too-tidy an origin story** — see Origin/Founder Story Structure; authenticity beats polish here specifically.
