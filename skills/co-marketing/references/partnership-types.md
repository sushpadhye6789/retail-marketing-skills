# Partnership Types — A Retail Taxonomy

The main skill file's Partner Identification Framework and Campaign Types tables carry over a software-industry framing (integration ecosystems, app marketplace categories, account-overlap tools like Crossbeam) that doesn't map cleanly onto retail. A retail brand doesn't have an "integration partner" the way two SaaS products do — but it has several genuinely distinct partnership shapes, each with different mechanics, different partners worth approaching, and different ways to measure success. Name which type before brainstorming campaign ideas; the type determines almost everything else.

## The seven types

### 1. Co-branded product collaboration

Two brands create a limited-run product together — not a cross-promotion of two separate products, but one product that carries both names. The highest-effort, highest-payoff type on this list.

- **Examples:** A beverage brand and a snack brand releasing a paired limited flavor; two apparel brands releasing a joint capsule collection; a beauty brand and an artist/creator releasing a co-designed palette.
- **Best fit:** Brands with comparable prestige/audience size (a wildly mismatched collab reads as one brand borrowing the other's credibility) and genuine creative or product-development capacity to execute it.
- **What it needs:** A real production timeline (this is a product launch, not a marketing campaign — see `launch`), clear IP/revenue-split terms in writing before development starts, and a joint go-to-market plan across both audiences.
- **Measure:** Units sold, sell-through rate, audience overlap captured (new-to-file customers from the partner's audience), press/UGC generated.

### 2. Complementary-category bundle

Two brands whose products are used together, but sold separately, package or promote a bundle — no shared product development, just shared merchandising and promotion.

- **Examples:** A coffee brand bundling with a mug or ceramics brand; a skincare brand bundling with a beauty-tool brand; a pet-food brand cross-promoting with a pet-accessory brand.
- **Best fit:** Adjacent-but-non-competing categories with genuine usage overlap — the "before/after/alongside" test from the main skill file's Audience Overlap Analysis applies directly here.
- **What it needs:** A discount or bundling mechanic both sides agree on, a shared landing page or in-store display, and a clear split of who fulfills what.
- **Measure:** Bundle attach rate, incremental units for each partner's core product (see `attribution/references/incrementality-checkpoint.md` before crediting the bundle with sales either partner would have made anyway).

### 3. Ingredient / component brand partnership

One brand's product is a visible, named input into another's — the partnership is a "Made with [Partner]" callout rather than a joint product or a bundle.

- **Examples:** A specialty coffee roaster named on a bakery's menu; a fabric mill or yarn brand named on an apparel brand's care tag and marketing; a supplement brand's key ingredient sourced from and co-marketed with a named supplier.
- **Best fit:** When the input brand itself carries recognition or trust that transfers to the finished product (a well-known ingredient/material brand lending credibility) — weak fit when the input is a commodity with no name recognition to lend.
- **What it needs:** Co-branded packaging or menu/product-page callouts, and usually a supply agreement that predates the marketing partnership (this often starts as a sourcing relationship, not a marketing one).
- **Measure:** Harder to isolate directly — track via brand lift surveys or self-reported attribution ("chose this because of the [ingredient brand]") rather than expecting a clean conversion number.

### 4. Local business cross-promotion

Two independent local businesses (not necessarily in the same category) promote each other to their respective local customer bases — the retail-specific counterpart to the digital "integration partner" search.

- **Examples:** A boutique retailer cross-promoting with a nearby café or salon; a home-goods store partnering with a local interior designer; a new-store-opening partnering with an established neighboring business for a joint opening event (see `new-store-launch`).
- **Best fit:** Any brick-and-mortar or locally-rooted retailer — this is one of the highest-ROI partnership types for a physical location because both audiences are already geographically qualified.
- **What it needs:** Almost no formal agreement in most cases — a co-hosted event, mutual social shoutouts, a shared flyer/window display, or referral discounts each side honors. See `local-marketing` for the broader local-visibility context this sits inside.
- **Measure:** Foot traffic referred (ask "how did you hear about us"), redemption of any cross-referral offer.

### 5. Cause / nonprofit partnership

A brand partners with a nonprofit or cause organization — not primarily a sales mechanic, though it can carry one (a percentage-of-sales donation, a co-branded product where proceeds go to the cause).

- **Best fit:** Brands whose audience genuinely cares about the cause in question — a mismatched or opportunistic-looking cause partnership does more brand damage than good. See `brand-management` before committing to a cause partnership that isn't a natural fit.
- **What it needs:** A real commitment (not a one-off social post) and clear, verifiable terms on how proceeds/support actually reach the cause — vague "a portion of proceeds" claims invite scrutiny.
- **Measure:** Brand sentiment/lift more than direct sales; funds or support actually delivered to the cause is the credibility metric that matters most publicly.

### 6. Wholesale / trade co-marketing

A supplier and a retail account (or two suppliers sharing dealer/retailer relationships) co-fund and co-execute marketing aimed at the retail account's own customers — distinct from the consumer-facing partnership types above because the "partner" is a channel relationship, not a peer brand.

- **Best fit:** Any brand with an active B2B/wholesale channel (see `marketing-strategy` Section 16) — this is less "finding a partner" and more "co-marketing with the accounts already in the pipeline."
- **What it needs:** Co-op marketing funds and clear brand-standard guardrails for what the retail account can execute independently (see `supplier-funded-marketing`, `trade-and-dealer-enablement`).
- **Measure:** Account-level sell-through lift, not brand-level metrics — this partnership type is evaluated one account relationship at a time.

### 7. Creator / independent-brand collaboration

A partnership with an individual creator's own brand or product line — distinct from a paid influencer sponsorship (see `influencer-marketing`) because the creator is a genuine co-brand with their own audience and creative input, not a paid endorser of an existing product.

- **Examples:** A creator co-designing a product or collection under their own name; a creator's existing small brand cross-promoting with a larger retailer's platform.
- **Best fit:** When the creator has a real, distinct audience worth reaching and enough of a track record that the collaboration reads as a genuine partnership rather than a disguised ad.
- **What it needs:** Clear IP and compensation terms (royalty vs. flat fee vs. revenue share), and — because compensation is usually involved — disclosure requirements apply the same as any paid endorsement (see `compliance`).
- **Measure:** Same as co-branded product collaboration (type 1) if a product results; audience/reach metrics if the partnership is promotional only.

## Choosing a type before brainstorming

Naming the type first changes what "brainstorming partner campaigns" (the main skill file's next section) should even produce — a co-branded product collaboration needs a production roadmap, not a campaign-idea list; a local cross-promotion needs almost no formal process at all. Match the effort and formality in the main skill file's campaign-type tables to the partnership type here, not the other way around.
