# Avinash Kaushik

**Lens:** Most analytics dashboards report vanity metrics that answer "what happened" without ever answering "so what" — the discipline that actually improves a business is designing measurement around the *why*, not just accumulating more numbers.

## Public framework

Kaushik (former Analytics Evangelist for Google, author of the influential blog Occam's Razor) wrote **Web Analytics 2.0**, reframing web analytics from a numbers-reporting exercise into what he called the "art of online accountability and science of customer centricity." The framework's core recommendations: build an actionable measurement strategy tied to real business questions before choosing metrics, apply experimentation (testing) as a primary analytical technique rather than an occasional add-on, measure social and multichannel activity honestly rather than forcing it into last-click models it doesn't fit, and directly incorporate qualitative customer listening (surveys, feedback) alongside quantitative data rather than treating them as separate disciplines.

## Retail application

This is foundational-level grounding for `analytics` and `attribution`: Kaushik's "so what" discipline is a direct check against exactly the failure mode this repo's Goodhart's Law guardrail (in `marketing-loops`) warns about — a dashboard full of metrics that move without anyone asking whether they represent real customer value. For retail specifically, his push toward combining quantitative behavioral data with qualitative customer listening maps directly onto `customer-research` and the review-mining/survey work this repo's other skills already recommend.

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** Auditing whether an analytics setup answers real business questions or just accumulates metrics; building the discipline of pairing quantitative dashboards with qualitative customer listening.
**Blind spots:** *Web Analytics 2.0* predates the current AI-search and privacy-driven measurement landscape (cookieless tracking, AI-agent-mediated shopping) — the underlying "measure what matters, not what's easy" discipline holds, but specific tooling recommendations from the book are dated and need modern replacements.
