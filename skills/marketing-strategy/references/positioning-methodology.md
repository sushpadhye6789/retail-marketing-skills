# Positioning Methodology

A step-by-step method for filling in Sections 5 (Competitive Landscape) and 6 (Differentiation) of the marketing strategy document — for when "who are our competitors and why do we win" needs more rigor than free-text bullets.

The method below is adapted from April Dunford's positioning process (*Obviously Awesome*), which has become the standard reference for this kind of work. It's paraphrased and restructured here, not quoted — cite the book directly if the user wants primary source material.

Positioning answers one question: **out of everything a customer could do instead, why should they pick you, and who should be told that story?** It is not a tagline exercise — the tagline comes *after* positioning is settled, as an expression of it.

---

## The five steps

### Step 1: List true competitive alternatives

Not "who else sells software like ours" — **what would this customer actually do if you didn't exist?** For many products, the real alternative is a spreadsheet, a manual process, a competitor's product, or doing nothing at all.

Ask: "If you hadn't found us, what would you be using right now?" Get the literal answer, not the category-mate answer. A project management tool's real alternative for a two-person team is often "a shared doc and Slack messages," not another project management tool.

**For ecommerce/DTC specifically:** the alternative is frequently a specific named competitor product on the same shelf (physical or digital), a cheaper generic version, or "not buying this category at all" (e.g., a supplement's real alternative might be "nothing — I just deal with the problem"). Don't skip "doing nothing" as a real alternative; it's often the biggest one.

### Step 2: List your unique attributes

What do you have that the alternatives from Step 1 don't? Be literal and specific — features, capabilities, ingredients, process, team, supply chain, anything factually true about you that isn't true of the alternatives.

This is a long, unfiltered list at this stage. Don't judge yet — just capture everything that's actually different.

### Step 3: Map attributes to value

For each unique attribute, answer "so what?" until you reach something the customer actually cares about. An attribute is a fact about the product; a value is a change in the customer's life or business.

| Attribute (fact about you) | So what? | Value (what the customer gets) |
|---|---|---|
| "Same-day formulation testing in-house" | → so it ships faster | → "New product ideas go from concept to shelf in weeks, not months" |
| "Cold-pressed, single-origin sourcing" | → so the ingredient is less processed | → "Visibly better skin results in 2 weeks, not 8" |

Attributes that don't survive the "so what?" chain to a real customer value aren't differentiators — they're trivia. Cut them.

### Step 4: Identify who cares a lot

Not everyone values the same attributes equally. The customers who care *most* about your specific values are your best-fit segment — the ones where your differentiation is the deciding factor, not a nice-to-have.

Characterize this segment concretely: not "people who want good skincare" but "people who've already tried 2-3 products in this category and were disappointed by slow or invisible results" — a segment defined by what they've already tried and what they were let down by is far more targetable than one defined by demographics alone.

### Step 5: Determine market category

The category you claim is the *frame* the customer uses to evaluate you. A wrong category creates wrong comparisons: calling a $180 candle "a candle" invites price comparison against $15 candles; calling it "a home fragrance experience" or positioning it next to fine home goods changes the comparison set entirely.

Two failure modes:
- **Category too narrow/unfamiliar** — customers don't know how to evaluate something with no reference point, so they don't buy at all
- **Category too broad/generic** — customers apply the wrong comparison set (usually price) and your differentiation gets ignored

Pick the category that makes your Step 3 values the *obvious* basis for comparison, not an afterthought.

## Worked example: ecommerce/DTC

**Product:** A $68 vitamin C serum.

1. **Alternatives:** Other vitamin C serums on the shelf (mid-market and prestige), doing nothing about the skin concern, a dermatologist visit for a prescription retinoid.
2. **Unique attributes:** Stabilized 15% L-ascorbic acid (most competitors use derivatives that are gentler but less potent), small-batch production with a printed batch date on every bottle, formulated with a dermatologist who publishes the clinical study.
3. **Attributes → value:** Stabilized high-potency vitamin C → visible brightening in 14 days, not 8 weeks. Batch-dated small production → the product hasn't been sitting in a warehouse degrading (vitamin C oxidizes — freshness is a real, checkable claim, not marketing fluff). Dermatologist-published study → the "will this actually work for me" objection gets a real answer, not a testimonial.
4. **Who cares most:** People who've already bought 1-2 vitamin C serums that didn't visibly do anything — they're not new to the category, they're disappointed by it.
5. **Category:** Not "skincare" (too broad, invites price comparison against drugstore brands) and not "clinical dermatology" (too narrow, sounds intimidating and expensive). Land on "clinically-proven active skincare" — narrow enough to signal potency, broad enough to still be a familiar shelf.

## Worked example: B2B/Wholesale

**Product:** An activewear line selling wholesale to independent boutique fitness studios.

1. **Alternatives:** A well-known national activewear brand the studio already stocks, a print-on-demand branded-merch vendor for studio logo gear, doing nothing and letting members buy activewear elsewhere.
2. **Unique attributes:** Studio-exclusive colorways not sold direct-to-consumer, a 30-day sale-or-return trial with no minimum reorder commitment, size-inclusive fit (XS-3XL in every style, not just core sizes).
3. **Attributes → value:** Studio-exclusive colorways → the studio can market it as "our own line," not just a rack of the same brand every other studio carries. Sale-or-return trial → the studio owner can stock it with near-zero financial risk instead of tying up cash in a full case order. Size-inclusive fit → the studio doesn't lose sales from members who don't fit the brand's core size run, a real and recurring complaint about the national brand.
4. **Who cares most:** Independent studio owners currently stocking the national brand at thin margin who want a retail line that feels differentiated to their members — not studios with no retail program at all, who need a different, more basic pitch.
5. **Category:** Not "activewear wholesale" (too broad, invites price comparison against the national brand on the same terms) and not "studio merch" (too narrow, sounds like logo gear, not real apparel). Land on "studio-exclusive retail program" — specific enough to signal the differentiation and the low-risk trial mechanic, not just another case of inventory.

---

## When positioning work is done

You have a real positioning statement when you can fill this in without hedging:

> For [target market characteristics from Step 4], [product] is the [market category from Step 5] that [key value from Step 3], because [unique attribute from Step 2] — unlike [primary alternative from Step 1].

If any blank makes you want to add "sort of" or "mostly," the corresponding step needs more work before moving on.

Feed the results back into Sections 5 and 6 of the marketing strategy document. The document doesn't need the full worked derivation — just the conclusions (competitive alternatives, differentiators, why customers choose you) — but doing the derivation is what makes those conclusions defensible instead of asserted.
