# Planogram & POS Compliance Guide

Shared by `visual-merchandising` and `pos-marketing` — the plan-vs-shelf reference `marketing-strategy`'s guides are for strategy. Both skills already name the problem this closes: a planogram that "looks right on paper" isn't the same as what's actually on the shelf, and POS materials "produced but never actually placed" are a common, easy-to-miss failure. This is the method for checking, not just knowing the risk exists.

## Why This Matters More Than It Looks

A planogram or POS kit is a set of *instructions* sent to a location — corporate-owned, franchised, or dealer-executed. Nothing about sending it guarantees it was followed. The gap between plan and shelf is where most in-store marketing spend quietly evaporates: a hero display never assembled, a reset that happened in the flagship store but not the other twelve, a discount sign still up two weeks after the promo ended. None of this shows up in a digital dashboard — it only shows up if someone actually looks.

## The Audit Method (No Special Hardware Required)

Designed to work from a smartphone, for a single-location business and a multi-location/dealer footprint alike — the small-retailer differentiator this repo aims for throughout: immediate, low-cost, no vendor tooling required to start.

**1. Define what "compliant" means before auditing anything.** A planogram or POS kit needs to specify: which fixture, which products, what facing count, what signage, by what date. An audit against a vague plan ("make it look nice") can't produce a real score — fix the plan's specificity first if this isn't already true.

**2. Photograph the same four angles every time.** Full fixture/display straight-on, close-up of the top-selling or hero-featured product's facing, close-up of any required signage, and a wide shot showing the display in the context of the surrounding zone (per `visual-merchandising`'s Store Zoning section — a technically correct display in the wrong zone still fails the plan's intent). Consistent angles make period-over-period comparison possible; a single photo tells you today, a consistent series tells you the trend.

**3. Score against the plan on four dimensions**, each a simple yes/no/partial:
   - **Placement** — is it in the specified zone/fixture, not just somewhere in the store?
   - **Facing/assortment** — do the products and facing counts match the plan, not just "something from that category"?
   - **Signage** — is the required signage present, current, and undamaged (not a leftover from the previous promotion)?
   - **Freshness** — is this still the *current* plan, or a stale reset that should have been refreshed by now (see Reset Cadence in `visual-merchandising`)?

**4. Convert scores to a simple compliance rate**, not just a pass/fail per location: (locations fully compliant) / (locations audited). A rate below roughly 70% across a dealer network points at a process problem (unclear instructions, insufficient lead time, no accountability) more than a series of individual failures — fix the process, not just the next reset.

**5. Close the loop.** A non-compliant location needs a specific, dated fix request — "reset the endcap to the March planogram by [date], missing: hero SKU facing, promo header card" — not a general reminder. See `AGENTS.md`'s execution-receipt convention for logging the audit and the fix request as a Tier 2 follow-up when it involves dealer-facing communication.

## Cadence

Match audit frequency to reset frequency and stakes, not a fixed default:
- **Flagship / own stores**: can be audited more frequently since it's lower-friction (staff can self-audit as part of a routine walk).
- **Multi-location or dealer footprint**: photo-based audits from each location on the reset's own cadence (see `visual-merchandising`'s Reset Cadence & Governance) is more realistic than attempting every location, every week.
- **High-stakes windows** (a major tentpole campaign, a new product launch's first two weeks) justify a tighter audit cadence than routine — this is when compliance gaps cost the most.

## Common Failure Modes This Method Catches

- **The photo-op reset** — the display looks right in the launch-day photo sent to corporate, then drifts uncorrected for the rest of the reset period. A single audit at launch catches nothing about the following weeks; the cadence matters more than any one check.
- **Compliant fixture, wrong zone** — the planogram was followed to the letter on a fixture that's in the decompression zone or a low-traffic aisle, quietly wasting the display's intended impact. Placement scoring (step 3) catches this only if the auditor also checks against `visual-merchandising`'s zoning guidance, not just the fixture diagram in isolation.
- **Signage lag** — the product reset happened but old signage never came down (or new signage never went up), creating a mismatch that reads as an error to the shopper even though the underlying merchandising is correct.
