# Competitive Pressure & Price War Guide

What to do when a competitor starts undercutting on price, repeatedly and deliberately, rather than through the normal back-and-forth of category pricing. This is a different situation from routine competitive pricing checks (see `pricing`'s own competitive-monitoring section) — a price war is a sustained, escalating pattern that can destroy category-wide margin if every player just matches every move.

## Is it actually a price war?

Don't treat one aggressive promotion as a war. Check for the pattern first:

| Signal | Routine competition | Price war |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | Occasional promotional overlap (both running a seasonal sale) | Repeated, escalating undercuts specifically targeting your price points |
| **Depth** | Within normal category promotional range | Depth that isn't sustainable at normal margin — a signal the competitor is buying share, not just promoting |
| **Trigger** | A calendar event (EOFY, holiday) both categories run | A reaction to something specific — your launch, your own price move, a new entrant, an inventory dump |
| **Duration** | Time-bound, ends on schedule | Open-ended, no announced end date |

If it's routine, this guide doesn't apply — use `pricing`'s normal competitive-monitoring cadence instead.

## The core decision: match, hold, differentiate, or exit

Four responses exist. Which one is right depends on brand tier (`brand-tier-guide.md`) and your actual margin floor, not on how uncomfortable it feels to watch a competitor win share.

- **Match** — only defensible with a real, calculated margin floor already in hand (see `AGENTS.md`'s fail-closed rule: never match a competitor's price without first knowing what floor you can sustain). Matching without doing this math is the single most common way a price war becomes a solvency problem instead of a marketing problem.
- **Hold** — hardest to execute, easiest to recommend badly. Only defensible if paired with a real answer to "why should the customer still choose us at a higher price" — a differentiation story (`storytelling`, `copywriting`), a service/warranty advantage, or genuine product superiority the customer can verify. Holding price with no differentiation story is just losing share slowly instead of quickly.
- **Differentiate** — compete on value instead of price: bundling (`offers`), loyalty depth (`loyalty`), service quality, faster fulfillment, exclusive access. This is almost always the right first move for a Premium/Luxury tier brand (see `brand-tier-guide.md`) before ever considering matching.
- **Exit the fight** — walk away from the specific SKU or segment the war is being fought over, and reallocate budget/attention elsewhere (`budget-allocation`, `range-review`). A legitimate, sometimes underused option — not every battle is worth winning, especially on a low-margin SKU a competitor is using as a loss leader.

**Brand tier changes the default answer:**

| Tier | Default response |
|---|---|
| Budget | Matching is often survivable and sometimes necessary — the customer already expects "always on sale" pricing at this tier (see `brand-tier-guide.md`). Watch margin floor closely; a budget-tier price war is the fastest way to erode an already-thin margin. |
| Mid-market | Differentiate first; match selectively only on the specific SKUs where you're structurally advantaged (cost, supply, exclusivity). |
| Premium / Luxury | Matching is rarely the right call — a premium brand racing a discounter to the bottom damages the price-as-signal positioning itself, often permanently (see `brand-tier-guide.md`'s discounting-norm row). Differentiate or exit; hold only with real proof behind the price gap. |

## Fail-closed requirements before recommending a response

- **A margin floor, not an assumed one.** Don't recommend matching or even a partial discount without the actual number — see `discount-and-clearance` and `pricing` for how to derive it, and `AGENTS.md`'s fail-closed rule for why guessing here is worse than refusing to answer.
- **MAP/dealer-channel constraints, if they exist.** A price war fought on your own direct channel can create a MAP violation or channel-conflict problem on the dealer side without anyone intending it — see `marketing-strategy/references/distribution-model-guide.md`'s Discounting row before recommending a channel-wide price move.
- **A real end condition.** Any match/hold decision needs an explicit re-evaluation trigger (a date, a share-loss threshold, a margin threshold) — an undefined "match until they stop" commitment is how a temporary tactic becomes a permanent, unplanned repricing of the whole category.

## Non-price responses worth exhausting before cutting price

A price cut is often the least creative and most permanent response available. Before recommending one, check whether these solve the actual problem instead:

- **Bundling and value-stacking** (`offers`) — raise perceived value without touching the headline price.
- **Loyalty/status deepening** (`loyalty`) — make switching costlier for the customer than the price gap justifies.
- **Service and fulfillment differentiation** — faster shipping, better returns, real warranty/support advantage, communicated clearly (`copywriting`).
- **Messaging that reframes the comparison** (`storytelling`, `copywriting`) — a price war is partly a framing fight; a competitor's lower price only wins if the customer is comparing on price alone.
- **Reviewing whether the affected SKU is even strategically worth defending** (`range-review`) — sometimes the right move is deliberately ceding a low-margin, non-core SKU rather than defending it at any cost.

## What this guide doesn't cover

This is a marketing response framework, not a full competitive-strategy or antitrust review. A price war that involves predatory pricing, collusion signals, or other legal exposure is a `compliance`/legal matter first — flag it rather than proceeding on marketing tactics alone. And a price war severe enough to threaten the business's viability is a Section 12 (Strategic Priorities) conversation, not something this guide resolves on its own — escalate rather than treating a marketing response as sufficient.
