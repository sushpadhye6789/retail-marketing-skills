# Customer Research — Interviews & Surveys (Primary Research)

Going to the source. Mode 2 mines what customers already said in public; this is Mode 3 — you *ask*. Customer research is your marketing cheat code, and the highest-signal version is talking to customers directly.

Three primary-research pillars, best used together:
1. **Video/phone calls** — deep, unstructured, follow-the-thread (this file)
2. **Surveys** — broad, quantified, benchmarkable (this file)
3. **Online sleuthing** — digital watering-hole mining (see Mode 2 above and `references/source-guides.md`)

---

## The First Rule of Customer Research

> The first rule of customer research: you do not talk about customer research.

Keep it casual. The moment a customer thinks they're in "a research study" they perform — they give you the polished, socially-acceptable answer instead of the real one. Frame calls as a chat, not an interview. Don't lead. Don't pitch. Don't defend the product. You're there to listen and learn how they actually think, talk, shop, and decide.

**Prove yourself wrong, not right.** The point of research is not validation — it's disconfirmation. Go in trying to *break* your assumptions, not confirm them. If you only look for evidence you're right, you'll find it, and it'll be worthless.

- **Airbnb example**: the team assumed listings were underperforming on price or copy. Research aimed at disproving that assumption revealed the real problem was bad *photos* — hosts' own phone snapshots made great homes look shabby. Airbnb sent photographers to shoot host homes instead. Chasing the price/copy confirmation would have optimized the wrong lever entirely.
- Ask questions that could return an answer you don't want to hear. If none of your questions can prove you wrong, rewrite them.

## Digital Watering Hole Research (Mode 2, restated as a pillar)

See the Mode 2 section above for the full playbook — the same discipline applies here: read reviews, threads, and comments to mine **pains**, **jargon** (the exact words shoppers use), **recommendations** (what they tell each other to buy or avoid), and **worldview**. It's passive (you observe) where interviews are active (you ask). Run it first: it tells you what to ask about, and in whose words.

## Customer Interviews (Video/Phone Calls)

### Recruit your best customers

Don't interview whoever answers first. Interview the customers you want *more of*. Segment your customer base and prioritize by:

- **High order value / high lifetime spend** — the customers worth the most
- **Fast repeat purchase** — they "got it" fast; their language converts fast
- **Low churn / high retention** (see `retention-and-winback`) — they got real, lasting value from the product or the shopping experience

Recruitment methods, in order of leverage:
1. **Segment the CRM/email list** by the three signals above and pull a shortlist
2. **Ask store/CS teams for referrals** — they know who loves the product and who articulates why
3. **Always close every call with**: *"Who else should we talk to?"* — the single most reliable way to compound your interview pipeline

### Incentives

- **$25–50 gift card per call** (~20–30 min); **$5 per survey response** (retail's typically lower AOV than B2B SaaS means the incentive should scale to the category, not default to a fixed number)
- Aim for **10 calls, be happy with 5.** Signal saturates fast — by call 5-6 you'll hear the same themes repeat. Don't stall the project waiting for a perfect sample.
- Offer the incentive up front; it dramatically lifts response rate and shows you value their time. Store credit or gift cards both work — store credit doubles as a small retention nudge.

### Outreach email template

Keep it short, casual, specific, and low-commitment. Not a "research study."

```
Subject: Quick favor — 20 min, on us

Hi [First name],

I'm [name] from [company]. I'm trying to get better at making
[product category] work for customers like you, and I'd love to
steal 20 minutes to hear how [product/shopping experience] is
actually working for you — what's good, what's annoying, what you
wish were different. No pitch, no agenda.

As a thank you I'll send you a [$25/$50 gift card / store credit].

Are you free [day] or [day] this week? Here's my calendar: [link]

Thanks either way,
[Name]
```

Notes:
- "No pitch, no agenda" and "what's annoying" signal you actually want the truth.
- One clear ask, two concrete time options, a booking link. Remove friction.
- Never say "customer research study."

## Keep Asking Why (5-Why Laddering)

The first answer is never the real answer. **Keep Asking Why** — ladder each response down 3-5 levels until you hit the root motivation, the practical constraint, or the emotional driver. Surface answers are symptoms; the bottom of the ladder is why they buy and why they come back.

**Worked example** — laddering a lapsed-customer signal to the real churn cause:

- **Q: Why haven't you reordered since your last purchase?**
  - "I just haven't gotten around to it."
- **Why haven't you gotten around to it?**
  - "Honestly, last time it was out of stock in my size when I needed it."
- **Why did that matter enough to stop you reordering?**
  - "I ended up buying the same thing from [competitor] because they had it in stock that week."
- **Why did you stick with the competitor after that?**
  - "Once I'd already set up an account and it worked fine, there wasn't a strong reason to switch back."
- **Why is that the real risk?**
  - "If stock availability is what decided it, no amount of email marketing brings me back — the loyalty was never really about the brand, it was about who had it when I needed it."

The surface answer was "haven't gotten around to it." The root is a **stock-availability gap that quietly handed a loyal customer to a competitor**, and no win-back email fixes that — only inventory/replenishment planning does (see `retention-and-winback` and `range-review`). You can't fix "hasn't reordered." You can fix stock visibility at the moment of intended repurchase.

**Pain points vs. passion points.** Ladder for both. Pain points are what's broken and what they'll switch brands to escape. Passion points are what they love, brag about, and would be genuinely disappointed to lose. Passion points drive retention and referrals; pains drive churn and lost acquisition. Capture both in their words.

## Surveys

### The PMF Survey (Sean Ellis / Superhuman), adapted for retail

The single most useful survey question, from Sean Ellis and popularized by Superhuman's Rahul Vohra — it transfers to a product/brand just as well as to software:

> **"How would you feel if you could no longer buy [product/brand]?"**
> - Very disappointed
> - Somewhat disappointed
> - Not disappointed
> - N/A — I no longer buy it

**The 40% benchmark**: if **40% or more** of customers answer **"very disappointed,"** you likely have strong product-market fit. Below 40%, keep iterating on the product, the assortment, or the experience.

Run it as a recurring pulse, not once. Follow the core question with:
- *"What type of person do you think would most benefit from [product/brand]?"* (sharpens the ICP/persona)
- *"What is the main benefit you get from [product/brand]?"* (your positioning, in their words)
- *"How can we improve [product/brand] for you?"* (roadmap and assortment fuel from fence-sitters)

Segment every answer by the "very disappointed" cohort vs. the rest — that cohort is your true core customer, and the one worth building lookalike/retention strategy around.

### Survey design guardrails

- Keep it short — every extra question drops completion.
- Prefer open-ended for language mining; multiple-choice answers are artifacts of the options you gave.
- Don't lead. A question that telegraphs the answer you want returns the answer you want, not the truth.
- $5/response incentive lifts completion; deliver it on submit (a discount code works as well as cash for a retail audience).

## Case Anchors

- **Airbnb (host photography)**: research revealed listings failed because the *photos* were bad, not the pricing or copy. Sending photographers to shoot host homes was a fix nobody would have guessed without talking to the market. Research points at problems you can't see from inside.
- **Superhuman (PMF survey)**: engineered the roadmap around the "very disappointed" metric — the same method applies to a retail assortment or a flagship product line.
