# Mark Ritson

**Lens:** Brand-building and short-term sales activation aren't competing budget lines — they're asymmetric partners, and most marketers under-invest in the long-term half because its payoff isn't visible next quarter.

## Public framework

Ritson teaches (via Marketing Week's Mini MBA) the **60/40 rule** originated by Les Binet and Peter Field: roughly 60% of budget to brand-building, 40% to short-term activation, as a category-level starting point — not a rigid formula. He's explicit that the ratio shifts by sector (Binet and Field's own later work shows ranges from ~80/20 in financial services to closer to 50/50 in B2B) and that the point isn't the exact split but the asymmetry underneath it: long-term brand advertising measurably lifts short-term sales performance, while short-term activation alone doesn't build the brand equity that makes future activation cheaper — what Ritson calls "the ultimate strategic BOGOF" (buy long-term, get short-term free).

## Retail application

This maps directly onto a tension this repo's `budget-allocation` and `discount-and-clearance` skills already navigate: a retailer leaning too hard into promotional/discount activity because it's the easiest thing to measure is trading long-term brand equity for a short-term number, and the 60/40 framing gives that trade-off a name and a starting ratio to defend or deliberately deviate from (with a stated reason) rather than drifting into all-activation by default.

## Best for / blind spots

**Best for:** Justifying a brand-building budget line against pressure to fund only measurable, short-term activation; sanity-checking whether a marketing mix has drifted too far toward promotion.
**Blind spots:** The 60/40 figure is a category-level heuristic from Binet and Field's aggregate data, not a rule for any single business — sector, competitive set, and current brand strength all shift the right ratio, and Ritson himself frames it that way rather than as fixed doctrine.
