# Vertical Nuance Guide

How a business's product category changes the *default* marketing tactics other skills in this repo recommend. This is a separate axis from brand tier (`brand-tier-guide.md`) and distribution model (`distribution-model-guide.md`) — a business is any vertical at any tier through any channel, but the vertical changes what's even relevant: perishability, return behavior, regulatory load, and promotional calendar all vary by category in ways that change the right default, not just the right example.

**Use the closest-fitting vertical and name the deviation** if a business genuinely straddles two (a grocery retailer that also sells small appliances, an apparel brand with a beauty line) — don't force a single label onto a business that legitimately spans categories. This mirrors `marketing-plan/references/client-types.md`'s "when the client doesn't fit cleanly" approach.

## The verticals

| Vertical | Core dynamic |
|---|---|
| **Fashion / Apparel** | Seasonal collections, high SKU/style turnover, size/fit uncertainty drives returns |
| **Grocery / Food & Beverage** | Perishability, high purchase frequency, low consideration per item, thin margins |
| **Beauty & Personal Care** | Repeat/replenishment-driven, ingredient/claims sensitivity, sampling matters disproportionately |
| **Electronics & Appliances** | Considered purchase, spec/comparison-driven research, warranty/support is a real differentiator |
| **Hardware, Tools & Trade** | Dual audience (consumer DIY + professional trade), dealer/trade-channel heavy, durability/reliability is the core claim |
| **Home Goods & Furniture** | High AOV, low purchase frequency, shipping/delivery logistics are part of the product experience |

This isn't an exhaustive list of every retail category — it's a representative spread covering the dimensions that most change downstream guidance. A business in an unlisted category (pet supplies, toys, sporting goods, etc.) should map itself to the closest-fitting row on each dimension below rather than needing its own row.

## What changes by vertical

### Perishability & inventory risk (see `overstock`, `discount-and-clearance`, `range-review`)

| Vertical | What changes |
|---|---|
| Fashion / Apparel | Style/season perishability, not physical — an unsold winter coat isn't spoiled, but it's worth sharply less once the season passes. Clearance timing is calendar-driven, not shelf-life-driven. |
| Grocery / Food & Beverage | Genuine physical perishability on a large share of SKUs — inventory decisions run on a much shorter clock than any other vertical here, and "clearance" often means today, not this quarter. Waste/shrinkage is a real cost `overstock`'s standard framing doesn't have to account for elsewhere. |
| Beauty & Personal Care | Shelf-life exists (expiry dates, formulation stability) but is usually measured in months to a few years, not days — a middle case between fashion's seasonal perishability and grocery's physical perishability. |
| Electronics & Appliances | Technical obsolescence risk (a new model release makes the old one much harder to sell at full price) rather than physical decay — closer in shape to fashion's seasonal risk than grocery's. |
| Hardware, Tools & Trade | Low perishability, low obsolescence risk relative to the other verticals — a well-made durable good doesn't lose relevance on a fixed clock the way a fashion item or a phone does. Inventory risk here is more about a discontinued model/platform than time itself (see `product-lifecycle`). |
| Home Goods & Furniture | Low perishability, but high carrying cost per unit (large, expensive to store and to move) — overstock risk is a cash-flow and warehousing problem more than an obsolescence one. |

### Returns behavior (see `post-purchase-experience`, `cro`)

| Vertical | What changes |
|---|---|
| Fashion / Apparel | Highest return rates in retail, structurally — size/fit uncertainty is the main driver, not dissatisfaction with the product itself. A generous, low-friction return policy is often a genuine conversion driver here, not just a cost center. |
| Grocery / Food & Beverage | Return rates are structurally low (you don't return groceries the normal way) — refund/replacement for a damaged or incorrect item is the relevant flow, not a fit-driven return. |
| Beauty & Personal Care | Returns are complicated by hygiene/opened-product policies that don't apply elsewhere — many retailers can't resell an opened cosmetic or skincare item, which changes both the return policy itself and the sampling strategy (see below) used to reduce fit/shade-mismatch returns before they happen. |
| Electronics & Appliances | Moderate return rates, often driven by "didn't work as expected" or buyer's remorse on a considered purchase rather than fit — a clear spec/comparison presentation upfront (see `product-feed`, `copywriting`) reduces this more than a generous policy does. |
| Hardware, Tools & Trade | Lower consumer-side return rates than fashion or electronics; on the trade/dealer side, returns are handled through a different mechanism entirely (stock rotation, warranty claims, defective-unit exchange) — see `trade-and-dealer-enablement`'s Returns & Deductions Management. |
| Home Goods & Furniture | Returns are logistically expensive regardless of rate (freight, white-glove pickup) — a wrong-fit large item costs far more to reverse than a wrong-fit shirt, which pushes more weight onto pre-purchase decision support (room visualizers, detailed dimensions, honest photography) than onto a generous return policy alone. |

### Regulatory & compliance load (see `compliance`)

| Vertical | What changes |
|---|---|
| Fashion / Apparel | Moderate — care labeling, fiber-content disclosure, and (for children's apparel) safety standards; sustainability/ethical-sourcing claims need the same substantiation discipline `compliance` applies to any comparative or guarantee claim. |
| Grocery / Food & Beverage | High — ingredient/allergen disclosure, health/nutrition claims, food safety and handling regulations, and (for alcohol) age-verification and marketing restrictions. This vertical has the least room for a loosely-worded claim of any on this list. |
| Beauty & Personal Care | High — ingredient disclosure, claims substantiation is especially strict for anything implying a medical/therapeutic effect (a cosmetic claiming to "treat" a skin condition crosses into drug-claim territory in most markets), and animal-testing/cruelty-free claims need real substantiation if made. |
| Electronics & Appliances | Moderate-to-high — safety certifications (electrical standards vary by market), warranty-term disclosure requirements, and e-waste/take-back obligations in some jurisdictions. |
| Hardware, Tools & Trade | Moderate-to-high where professional/safety use is involved — safety-rating claims, warranty terms for professional-grade equipment, and (for anything used in regulated trades) compliance with the standards that trade operates under. |
| Home Goods & Furniture | Moderate — flammability and material-safety standards (especially upholstered furniture and children's items), and assembly/instruction accuracy where a safety issue could result from a manual's error. |

### Promotional calendar & discounting norms (see `discount-and-clearance`, `brand-tier-guide.md`)

| Vertical | What changes |
|---|---|
| Fashion / Apparel | Hard seasonal calendar (end-of-season clearance is near-universal and expected by the customer) — the question isn't whether to discount at season-end, it's how deep and how the transition to full-price next-season stock is staged. |
| Grocery / Food & Beverage | Promotional cadence is often weekly/rolling (a rotating set of items on promotion) rather than seasonal-event-driven — closer to a constant low hum of promotion on some part of the range than fashion's periodic sharp discounting. |
| Beauty & Personal Care | Gift-with-purchase and sampling-led promotion (see Sampling below) is at least as common as straight discounting — a beauty promotion often adds value rather than cutting price, differently from most other verticals here. |
| Electronics & Appliances | Concentrated around known retail calendar moments (Black Friday-style events, back-to-school) and around a new-model release cycle (clearing the outgoing model — see `product-lifecycle`'s Replacement scenario) rather than a constant promotional rhythm. |
| Hardware, Tools & Trade | Consumer-side promotion follows general retail calendar moments; trade/professional-side "promotion" more often takes the shape of dealer incentives, volume rebates, and co-op-funded campaigns (`trade-and-dealer-enablement`, `supplier-funded-marketing`) than a public discount. |
| Home Goods & Furniture | Concentrated around a small number of major shopping moments (major sale weekends, new-financial-year, moving-season peaks) given the high AOV and low purchase frequency — a business here runs far fewer, larger promotional events than a grocery or beauty brand. |

### Sampling, trial, and pre-purchase reassurance (see `experiential-marketing`, `pos-marketing`)

| Vertical | What changes |
|---|---|
| Fashion / Apparel | Fit is the main uncertainty — size guides, fit-predictor tools, and a low-friction return policy substitute for physical trial in an online context. |
| Grocery / Food & Beverage | In-store sampling (tasting) is a long-standing, high-converting tactic specific to this vertical — see `experiential-marketing`'s In-Store Demo section. |
| Beauty & Personal Care | Sampling is disproportionately important here — a shade/scent/formulation mismatch is the main driver of both non-purchase and returns, and free/deluxe samples are a core acquisition and reassurance tactic, not just a nice-to-have. |
| Electronics & Appliances | Trial is harder to offer directly; specification clarity, comparison content, and credible third-party review seeding (`influencer-marketing`, `public-relations`) substitute for hands-on trial. |
| Hardware, Tools & Trade | In-person demo (dealer-hosted or trade-show) is a named, real conversion driver for professional buyers specifically — performance under real working conditions matters more than a spec sheet alone (see `experiential-marketing`). |
| Home Goods & Furniture | Room visualizers (AR/3D placement tools), detailed dimension/material information, and (for higher-end retailers) in-showroom experience substitute for the trial a smaller, cheaper item wouldn't need. |

## How other skills should use this field

If `.agents/marketing-strategy.md` names a vertical (or the closest-fitting one, per the guidance above), check the relevant row before recommending a tactic that assumes a different category's default — a clearance cadence, a returns policy stance, a compliance checklist, or a promotional calendar all have a materially different right answer depending on vertical. If no vertical is captured yet, ask directly rather than defaulting to whichever vertical happens to be the most commonly discussed one — this repo's guidance should generalize across categories, not silently assume one.

This file doesn't need to be read end-to-end for every task — only the row relevant to the tactic in question, the same way `distribution-model-guide.md` is used.
