# Worked Examples — Weak vs. Strong

Two examples, each showing the sections that benefit most from real methodology (Competitive Landscape, Differentiation, Strategic Priorities) before and after applying [positioning-methodology.md](positioning-methodology.md) and [prioritization.md](prioritization.md). The other sections (Product Overview, Customer Language, Brand Voice, etc.) are largely direct capture — worked examples add less there.

---

## Example 1: Ecommerce/DTC — supplement brand

### Weak version (first draft, unprompted)

> **Competitive Landscape**
> Direct: Other supplement brands on Amazon and Shopify.
> Secondary: Getting nutrients from food instead.
> Indirect: Not doing anything about it.
>
> **Differentiation**
> We use high-quality ingredients and third-party testing. Great customer service.
>
> **Strategic Priorities**
> 1. Grow Instagram following
> 2. Improve product photography
> 3. Start a podcast sponsorship program
> 4. Launch on Amazon
> 5. Build a loyalty program
> 6. Improve email flows

This is generic — "high-quality ingredients" and "great customer service" are what every supplement brand claims, and the priorities list is six unranked ideas with nothing cut.

### Strong version (after running the methodology)

> **Competitive Landscape** *(via positioning-methodology.md)*
> - Direct alternative: [Named competitor] — same category, similar price, but uses a proprietary blend that hides exact dosages
> - Secondary alternative: Whole-food sourcing (diet changes) — slower, but perceived as "more natural" by our exact target buyer
> - Indirect alternative: Doing nothing — the largest alternative by volume; most people in our target segment have tried and abandoned at least one supplement before
>
> **Differentiation**
> - Attribute: every batch's exact dosage and third-party lab result is on a QR code on the bottle (not just "third-party tested" as a claim)
> - Value: buyers who've been burned by proprietary-blend brands that hide actual dosages get a checkable answer, not a trust-me claim
> - Who cares most: people who've already bought and abandoned at least one supplement in this category because they weren't sure it was "really" in there
> - Category: not "supplements" (invites price comparison against the cheapest options) — "verified-dose supplements," specific enough to make the QR-code transparency the obvious point of comparison
>
> **Strategic Priorities** *(via prioritization.md)*
> Binding constraint: Repeat-purchase rate — new customer acquisition is healthy, but most buyers don't reorder, meaning CAC never gets recovered past the first sale.
>
> 1. **Post-purchase email/SMS flow overhaul** — directly addresses the constraint; current flow has generic "did you know" content instead of usage reminders and reorder timing. High confidence (we have open/click data showing the current flow underperforms benchmark).
> 2. **Subscribe & Save relaunch with a named program** — same constraint from a structural angle; today it's an unlabeled checkbox at checkout with low opt-in. Moderate confidence, high reach (touches every checkout).
>
> Explicitly deprioritized:
> - Instagram growth — real opportunity, but an acquisition play; the constraint is downstream of acquisition, so new followers would land in the same leaky repeat-purchase funnel.
> - Amazon launch — a real distribution decision but a different kind of bet (channel strategy) that doesn't address why *existing* customers aren't returning; revisit after repeat-purchase rate improves.
> - Podcast sponsorships — same acquisition-vs-retention issue as Instagram.

## Example 2: B2B/Wholesale — a specialty food line pitching independent grocers

### Weak version (first draft, unprompted)

> **Competitive Landscape**
> Direct: Other specialty sauce brands.
> Secondary: Store-brand/private-label options.
> Indirect: Customers not buying anything in this category.
>
> **Differentiation**
> We're higher quality and taste better than competitors.
>
> **Strategic Priorities**
> 1. Trade show presence
> 2. Outbound cold-email to buyers
> 3. Social media growth
> 4. Retail media/co-op ads
> 5. Rebrand packaging
> 6. Expand SKU count
> 7. Improve line sheet design

"Higher quality and tastes better" is an unfalsifiable claim every competitor also makes. Seven priorities, no constraint named, nothing cut.

### Strong version (after running the methodology)

> **Competitive Landscape** *(via positioning-methodology.md)*
> - Direct alternative: [Named competitor brand] — similar flavor profile and price point, but requires a 500-unit minimum order most independent grocers can't commit to on an unproven line
> - Secondary alternative: Store-brand/private-label — cheaper, already on shelf, but doesn't carry the "discovered a great small brand" story that drives repeat trips to a specialty grocer
> - Indirect alternative: Not stocking anything new in this category — the largest alternative; most buyers only add a new line when a genuine gap or a customer request forces the decision
>
> **Differentiation**
> - Attribute: a 12-unit case minimum with a 30-day sale-or-return trial period, plus a "what's selling" monthly report showing sell-through by SKU
> - Value: a buyer without the shelf space or risk appetite for a 500-unit commitment can trial the line at near-zero downside, and self-diagnose which SKUs to reorder instead of guessing
> - Who cares most: independent/specialty grocers with 1-3 locations who've been burned before by a slow-moving case of an untested brand — big enough to want new discovery items, not big enough to absorb dead stock
> - Category: not "gourmet sauce" (undersells the low-risk trial mechanic) and not "artisanal condiments" (implies a farmers-market-only positioning, not a real wholesale line) — "low-risk trial-to-shelf specialty line," specific enough to signal the buying mechanic is the differentiator, not just the product
>
> **Strategic Priorities** *(via prioritization.md)*
> Binding constraint: Buyer risk tolerance — trade show interest is strong, but conversations stall at the order-commitment stage because a 500-unit-equivalent case minimum is too much risk for an unproven line at a small independent grocer.
>
> 1. **30-day sale-or-return trial with a 12-unit case minimum** — directly addresses the constraint; today, the standard case size is the stall point. High confidence (buyer conversations consistently cite minimum order size as the objection).
> 2. **"What's selling" monthly sell-through report as a standard trial deliverable** — lets a buyer see real velocity data before committing to a full reorder, shortening the trial-to-standing-order decision. Moderate confidence, high reach (works for any trial account, not just trade-show leads).
>
> Explicitly deprioritized:
> - Trade show presence — real long-term lever, but doesn't address why conversations *already happening* are stalling; revisit once the trial mechanic is live and proven.
> - Rebrand packaging — no evidence packaging/positioning is the issue; the stall point is order-commitment risk, not shelf appeal.
> - Retail media/co-op ads, SKU expansion — both are acquisition or range bets; same logic as trade shows, they don't fix the stall point.

---

## Pattern across both examples

| | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive Landscape | Generic category-mates | Real alternatives including "doing nothing," using the customer's actual language |
| Differentiation | Unfalsifiable claims ("high-quality," "easier to use") | Specific attribute → value chain, tied to a named segment who cares most |
| Strategic Priorities | 6-7 unranked ideas | 2 priorities tied to one named binding constraint, with an explicit, reasoned cut list |

In both cases, the weak version wasn't wrong exactly — it just wasn't a strategy. It was a list of things that would plausibly help. The strong version required naming what's actually constraining growth and showing the work of choosing against that constraint, not just describing the business.
